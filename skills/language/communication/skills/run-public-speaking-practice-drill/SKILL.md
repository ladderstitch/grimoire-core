# Run Public Speaking Practice Drill

Build public-speaking fluency and confidence through a repeated cycle of prepared speeches, extemporaneous speaking practice, and structured feedback — the same core format Dale Carnegie's Effective Speaking course and Toastmasters International independently converged on — rather than trying to improve by giving occasional real presentations with no deliberate practice structure.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Adopted by:** Dale Carnegie's Effective Speaking course, running continuously since 1912, and Toastmasters International, founded 1924 and now operating in over 140 countries, independently arrived at the same core practice cycle: prepared speeches built around specific structural techniques, extemporaneous speaking practice under time pressure, and structured peer/instructor feedback after every speech. Berkshire Hathaway has sponsored Dale Carnegie courses for its employees, and Warren Buffett has publicly credited taking the course early in his career with transforming his ability to speak in public — by his own account, he had been unable to speak to a group at all before taking it.

**Impact:** Buffett has stated that developing public-speaking and communication ability materially increased his career value — describing it as a skill that can increase a person's professional value substantially, a claim he has tied directly to his own experience going from unable to speak publicly to comfortable presenting to large audiences (including Berkshire's own shareholder meetings, now attended by tens of thousands of people) after completing the course. Toastmasters' decades of continuous operation and the persistence of Dale Carnegie's course curriculum across more than a century are themselves evidence that this specific practice format, rather than unstructured real-world speaking exposure alone, is what participants and sponsoring organizations have found produces durable improvement.

**Why best:** General speaking exposure (giving occasional real presentations at work) provides inconsistent, low-frequency practice with no guaranteed feedback and no deliberate structure targeting specific weaknesses. This practice cycle instead front-loads deliberate, structured repetition — prepared speeches with specific technique assignments, extemporaneous speaking under the pressure of no preparation time, and structured feedback after each — in a lower-stakes setting before those skills are needed in high-stakes real speaking situations. This is different from `design-presentation-structure`, which designs the argument architecture of one specific presentation; this skill instead builds the underlying speaking capability itself, independent of any single presentation's content.

Sources: Carnegie, *The Quick and Easy Way to Effective Speaking* (1962); Toastmasters International structured meeting format; documented Berkshire Hathaway/Buffett account of the course's impact.

## Steps

### 1. Establish a recurring practice cadence, not occasional real-stakes speaking

Schedule practice sessions on a recurring basis (weekly, in the Toastmasters model) rather than relying on whatever real presentations happen to come up at work — the deliberate, structured cycle is what produces improvement, not raw speaking exposure alone.

### 2. Deliver a prepared speech built around a specific structural technique

Assign each prepared speech a specific technique to practice — an opening hook, a clear single-point structure, a personal narrative arc, a persuasive call to action — rather than a generic "give a talk on anything." Practicing one technique deliberately per speech builds skill faster than unfocused general speaking.

### 3. Practice extemporaneous speaking under time pressure

Include short, unprepared speaking exercises (Toastmasters' "Table Topics" format: a prompt given with no advance notice, a brief response required immediately) to build the separate skill of organizing thoughts and speaking coherently without preparation time — a different capability than delivering a rehearsed prepared speech.

### 4. Receive structured feedback after every speech, not just informal impressions

Use a consistent feedback structure after each speech — specific strengths, specific areas to improve, and one or two concrete suggestions for the next attempt — rather than vague praise or criticism. Structured, specific feedback is what lets a speaker target the next practice session at an actual weakness.

### 5. Track progress across sessions against specific, named skills

Keep a record of which specific skills (pacing, filler-word reduction, vocal variety, eye contact, structural clarity) have been targeted and improved across sessions, rather than treating each speech as an isolated event with no continuity.

### 6. Escalate to higher-stakes real speaking opportunities only after the practice cycle has built baseline comfort

Use the low-stakes practice environment to build comfort and technique before applying them in a genuinely high-stakes setting (a real presentation, `apply-command-presence`'s high-stakes-moment context) — the practice cycle exists specifically to build the underlying capability somewhere safer than the real event.

## Rules

- Assign a specific technique to each prepared speech rather than practicing generically — deliberate, targeted repetition builds skill faster than unfocused speaking practice.
- Practice extemporaneous speaking separately from prepared speeches — these are genuinely different skills, and prepared-speech practice alone doesn't build comfort with unprepared speaking.
- Give and require structured, specific feedback after every speech — vague praise or criticism doesn't give the speaker anything concrete to work on next.
- Maintain a recurring cadence rather than practicing only when a real presentation happens to be coming up — consistency, not raw exposure, produces durable improvement.

## Examples

**Trigger:** Someone wants to become comfortable presenting at work but currently avoids speaking opportunities due to anxiety and lack of practice.
→ Set up a recurring low-stakes practice cadence (a local Toastmasters club, or a self-organized peer practice group). Start with prepared speeches on comfortable, personal topics, targeting one specific technique each (a clear opening, a structured three-point argument). Add extemporaneous practice once comfortable with prepared speeches. Use structured feedback each time to identify what's actually holding delivery back — filler words, pacing, lack of eye contact — rather than a vague sense of "I need to get better."

**Trigger:** A manager wants to help a technically strong but nervous presenter build general speaking confidence before pushing them into higher-stakes client presentations.
→ Don't send them straight into the high-stakes presentation with no practice runway. Set up several rounds of lower-stakes prepared-speech and extemporaneous practice with structured feedback first, specifically targeting whatever weaknesses show up (rushed pacing under nerves, weak structure) — building baseline comfort before the real, higher-stakes event.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating occasional real presentations as sufficient practice.** Infrequent, high-stakes real speaking opportunities don't provide the deliberate, structured repetition that produces durable skill improvement — and the stakes make each one a poor place to experiment with a new technique.
- **Giving only vague feedback ("that was good!" or "needs work").** Feedback has to name a specific strength and a specific, actionable improvement to be useful for the next practice session.
- **Skipping extemporaneous practice entirely.** Prepared-speech skill and unprepared-speaking skill are genuinely different capabilities — practicing only prepared speeches leaves someone unprepared for the unrehearsed speaking situations (Q&A, impromptu remarks) that come up constantly in real settings.
- **Jumping straight to high-stakes real speaking without any lower-stakes practice runway.** This skips the entire point of a deliberate practice cycle — building comfort and technique somewhere safer before the real, high-consequence event.

## When NOT to Use

- When the actual need is designing one specific presentation's content and argument, not building general speaking capability — use `design-presentation-structure` for that instead.
- When someone is about to walk into a specific high-stakes moment right now and needs in-the-moment authority-setting technique, not a multi-session practice program — use `apply-command-presence` for that immediate situation.
- When speaking anxiety is severe enough to resemble a clinical anxiety disorder rather than ordinary unfamiliarity/lack of practice — that calls for professional mental-health support, not a speaking-practice program alone.
