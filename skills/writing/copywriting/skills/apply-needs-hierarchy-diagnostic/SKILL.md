# Apply Needs Hierarchy Diagnostic

Before shipping a message or product appeal pitched at an aspirational need level — esteem, status, self-actualization — check whether the specific target audience's more basic needs are actually adequately met in this context right now; a higher-level appeal underperforms when a more basic, unmet need is what's actually salient for that audience.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Adopted by:** Maslow's needs taxonomy (physiological, safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualization) is near-universally taught and used as a practical audience-diagnosis heuristic across marketing, HR, product, and UX practice — it remains one of the most widely referenced frameworks for thinking about what motivates a given audience at a given moment, independent of the debate over its stricter theoretical claims.

**Impact:** A message pitched at esteem or aspiration tends to underperform when the audience it's targeting is actually preoccupied with a more basic, currently-unmet need (financial security, safety, belonging) — the appeal isn't wrong in the abstract, it's simply not addressing what's actually salient for that audience in that moment. Diagnosing and correcting this mismatch redirects effort from polishing the wrong appeal to targeting the right one.

**Why best:** The practical value of the framework is diagnostic, not doctrinal: it gives a structured way to ask "which level of need is actually active for this specific audience right now?" before assuming a higher-level appeal will land. This must be qualified, though — Maslow's original strict-hierarchy claim, that a lower need must be *fully* satisfied before a higher one can motivate at all, has weak empirical support and is disputed in modern motivation research. Wahba & Bridwell's 1976 review and Tay & Diener's 2011 survey of 123 countries (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) both found need fulfillment correlates with wellbeing largely independently of strict sequential order — people commonly pursue and satisfy higher-level needs even when lower-level needs are not fully met. This skill deliberately encodes only the defensible, non-strict version: check current salience for this specific audience, don't assume a rigid universal sequence the evidence doesn't support.

**Why best (continued):** This is a different mechanism than `apply-jobs-to-be-done`, which treats functional, social, and emotional dimensions of a customer's "job" as simultaneously operative and additive — JTBD never asks whether a more basic dimension is currently unmet in a way that would suppress response to a higher one. This skill's distinguishing move is exactly that salience-gating check, applied before crafting or shipping the message.

Sources: Maslow, *Psychological Review* (1943); Wahba & Bridwell, *Organizational Behavior and Human Performance* (1976); Tay & Diener, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* (2011).

## Steps

1. **Identify the need level the message or appeal is actually pitched at.** State explicitly whether the appeal targets a basic need (safety, financial security), a belonging need (community, connection), or an aspirational need (esteem, status, self-actualization) — most messages have a dominant level even if not labeled as such.

2. **Diagnose which need level is actually salient for the specific target audience, in this specific context, right now.** This is an empirical question about the audience's actual current situation, not an assumption based on their demographic category — the same audience segment can have different salient needs depending on timing, recent events, or context.

3. **Check for a mismatch between the appeal's level and the audience's actually-salient level.** If the message is pitched at esteem or aspiration but the audience's currently-salient need is more basic (financial security, safety, belonging), flag this explicitly as a likely cause of underperformance — the appeal's craft quality isn't the problem, its targeted level is.

4. **Re-pitch the message at the level that's actually active for the audience, or adjust who the message targets.** Either shift the message's framing to address the audience's actually-salient need, or if the message must stay aspirational, target a different audience segment for whom that level is already the active one.

5. **Don't assume a rigid, universal ordering across all audiences and contexts.** Different individuals and contexts can have a different currently-salient need even within demographically similar audiences — this diagnostic requires checking the specific audience's specific situation, not applying a fixed formula.

## Rules

- Diagnose the audience's actually-salient need for this specific context — don't assume it from demographic category alone.
- Never treat the strict sequential-prepotency claim as literally true — the evidence doesn't support a rigid "lower needs must be 100% satisfied first" ordering; use this as a salience check, not an enforced sequence.
- When a mismatch is found between the appeal's level and the audience's salient need, the fix is re-pitching the message or retargeting the audience — not simply improving the aspirational message's craft quality, which doesn't address a level mismatch.

## Examples

**Trigger:** A premium product's marketing campaign, pitched heavily on status and aspiration, underperforms with a segment of its target audience despite strong creative execution.
→ Diagnose whether that specific segment's more basic needs (financial security, job stability) are actually salient right now — if the segment is currently preoccupied with economic uncertainty, an aspirational status appeal will underperform regardless of how well-crafted it is. Either re-pitch the message toward the segment's actually-salient need, or retarget the aspirational campaign toward a segment for whom status/aspiration is genuinely the active concern.

**Trigger:** An HR communication about a new professional-development program (an esteem/growth-oriented benefit) gets low engagement from a specific team.
→ Check whether that team currently has more basic, unmet concerns (job security during a reorg, workload/safety issues) that are more salient than professional growth right now. If so, the low engagement isn't about the program's value — it's a level mismatch, and addressing the more basic concern first (or waiting until it's resolved) is likely to matter more than re-promoting the development program.

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming a fixed, universal need level for an entire demographic segment.** The same audience segment can have a different currently-salient need depending on context and timing — check the specific situation, don't apply a fixed assumption.
- **Treating Maslow's strict sequential-prepotency claim as literally, universally true.** The evidence doesn't support a rigid ordering where higher needs are completely inert until lower ones are fully satisfied — use the framework as a salience diagnostic, not an enforced sequence.
- **Responding to underperformance by improving the aspirational message's craft instead of checking for a level mismatch.** If the actual problem is that the message is pitched at the wrong need level for this audience, better execution of the same appeal won't fix it.
- **Ignoring that individuals within the same segment can have different currently-salient needs.** Applying one blanket diagnosis to an entire audience without accounting for genuine variation within it can misdirect the fix.

## When NOT to Use

- When the audience's needs are already well understood through more specific, direct research (customer interviews, JTBD analysis) — this diagnostic is most useful as an initial check, not a replacement for direct audience research where that's already available.
- For messaging where the appeal level genuinely doesn't matter to the outcome (purely functional, transactional communication with no aspirational or identity dimension) — the diagnostic adds no value when there's no need-level appeal to check in the first place.
- As a basis for claiming a rigid, universal sequence of need satisfaction applies to any specific individual — the qualifying evidence specifically argues against treating this as a strict, literal law.
