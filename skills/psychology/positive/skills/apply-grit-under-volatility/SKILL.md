# Apply Grit Under Volatility

When pursuing a long-term goal that involves sustained exposure to setbacks and volatility, treat persistence itself as the decisive variable — because the population still competing thins out specifically as difficulty continues, not because the pursuit stops being viable, and genuine grit is what separates people who eventually succeed from the much larger group who exit early.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Why best:** Grit is not simply "trying hard" or "having willpower" in a generic sense — Duckworth's research operationalizes it specifically as the combination of sustained passion and perseverance toward a long-term goal, measured independently of talent or initial ability, and found it to be a distinct predictor of eventual achievement across multiple demanding, attrition-heavy contexts. The mechanism this creates has a specific, falsifiable structural consequence: because most people who attempt a difficult, long-duration goal quit during the difficulty itself — not because the goal became provably unattainable — the population still actively competing shrinks over time, and persistence alone shifts a person's relative position in that thinning field, independent of any improvement in their absolute skill.

**Duckworth, Peterson, Matthews & Kelly (2007) and Duckworth, "Grit" (2016):** Duckworth's research program, including a landmark study of West Point cadets during "Beast Barracks" summer training, found that a validated grit measure predicted which cadets would complete the demanding program better than the military's own selection criteria (including physical fitness scores and academic performance), specifically because dropout during that program was driven by cadets' choice to quit under sustained difficulty rather than by an inability to meet an objective performance bar. The research replicated the same pattern — grit predicting persistence and eventual achievement independent of raw talent — across National Spelling Bee competitors, sales employees, and teachers in challenging school environments.

**DALBAR, "Quantitative Analysis of Investor Behavior" (annual):** DALBAR's long-running research consistently finds individual investors earn substantially lower returns than the market indices they're invested in, and attributes the gap specifically to behavioral mistiming — investors selling during periods of high volatility and drawdown, then re-entering after much of the subsequent recovery has already occurred. This provides a specific, quantified, real-world instance of the mechanism this skill targets: the investors who remain through volatility without panic-selling capture returns that a large share of the initial population forfeits by exiting during the difficult period.

**Adopted by:** Duckworth's grit research and assessment framework has been adopted in selection and training contexts across the US military (including West Point), and her subsequent public work has been widely incorporated into corporate leadership-development and educational psychology curricula; DALBAR's investor-behavior research is standard reference material in financial advising and behavioral finance practice, cited specifically to counsel clients against panic-selling during market volatility.
**Impact:** Duckworth's West Point study found a grit measure outperformed the institution's own established selection criteria in predicting who would complete a demanding program, isolating perseverance as a distinct, measurable predictor of completion independent of prior-assessed talent; DALBAR's annual research consistently documents a multi-percentage-point gap between average investor returns and the market indices they're invested in, attributed to behavioral exits during volatility — direct, quantified evidence that persistence through difficulty, not superior initial skill or timing, is what separates those who eventually capture the available outcome from those who don't.

## Steps

1. **Before starting a long-duration, high-difficulty pursuit, expect sustained setbacks and discouragement as the normal condition, not a sign the pursuit is failing.** Setting this expectation in advance prevents treating ordinary, predictable difficulty as new information that the goal is unviable.

2. **Distinguish discouragement based on genuine new evidence from discouragement based on the pursuit simply being hard.** Other people, including credible experts, will often advise quitting based on the general difficulty or base rate of failure in a category — this is different from specific, new evidence that your particular pursuit has become genuinely unviable; only the latter should change your course.

3. **Recognize that the population still actively competing thins out as difficulty continues — and that this is happening regardless of whether the pursuit remains viable.** Since most exits during a difficult period are driven by discouragement rather than proof of unviability, continuing to compete, on its own, improves your relative position among those still in the field.

4. **During periods of acute volatility or setback specifically, default to a pre-committed plan rather than a decision made in the moment.** Per the DALBAR research, exits during acute volatility are disproportionately behavioral rather than evidence-based — decide your response to volatility in advance, while calm, rather than re-deciding under the emotional pressure of the moment.

5. **Track the actual evidence for whether the pursuit remains viable, separately from your emotional state during setbacks.** Maintain a running, written record of the specific, factual signals that would indicate genuine unviability, and check the current situation against that record rather than against how discouraged you currently feel.

6. **Pair grit with periodic, honest reassessment — persistence is not the same as ignoring genuine disconfirming evidence.** Sustained perseverance through ordinary difficulty is the target behavior; persisting past clear, specific evidence that the goal is genuinely unreachable is a different failure mode this skill does not endorse.

## Rules

- Treat generic discouragement based on category-level difficulty or base rates as distinct from specific evidence that your particular pursuit has become unviable — only the latter should change your course.
- Decide your response to expected future volatility or setbacks in advance, while not currently experiencing them, rather than re-deciding in the moment under emotional pressure.
- Maintain a written, factual record of what would constitute genuine evidence of unviability, and check current conditions against that record rather than against your current emotional state.
- Persistence through ordinary difficulty is the goal; persistence past clear, specific disconfirming evidence is a distinct failure mode, not an application of this skill.

## Examples

**Entrepreneurship:** A founder repeatedly hears from experienced investors that most ventures in their category fail and is advised to abandon the idea. Applying this distinction, the founder checks whether the advice is based on general category-level base rates (which don't disqualify their specific venture) or on specific, new evidence about their particular business — finding it's the former, they continue, consistent with Duckworth's finding that grit, not immunity to discouraging feedback, predicts completion in demanding pursuits.

**Investing through volatility:** An investor holding a long-term position experiences a sharp, painful drawdown and feels strong pressure to sell. Having pre-committed, before the volatility occurred, to a specific set of factual conditions that would justify selling — none of which are currently met — the investor holds the position rather than deciding in the moment, consistent with the DALBAR research showing that investors who exit during volatility disproportionately forfeit the subsequent recovery.

**Skill acquisition:** Someone learning a difficult skill experiences a prolonged plateau and considers quitting, generalizing from others who gave up at a similar stage. Checking their own specific progress record rather than relying on how discouraged they currently feel, they find no genuine evidence their specific effort has stalled permanently, and continue — consistent with the pattern that most attrition happens during ordinary difficulty, not because further progress has become provably impossible.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating generic, category-level discouragement ("most people who try this fail") as specific evidence that your particular pursuit is unviable.** These are different kinds of information, and only the latter should change your course.
- **Making a decision to quit during the emotional peak of a setback, rather than checking it against a plan made in advance.** Decisions made during acute difficulty are the least reliable point at which to reassess viability.
- **Confusing grit with ignoring genuine disconfirming evidence.** Persisting through ordinary difficulty is the target behavior; persisting past clear, specific evidence the goal has become genuinely unreachable is a distinct, unendorsed failure mode.
- **Failing to maintain any factual record of viability, leaving the current emotional state as the only available input to a stay-or-quit decision.** Without a written record to check against, discouragement in the moment gets mistaken for actual evidence.

## When NOT to Use

- When specific, new, factual evidence indicates the pursuit has genuinely become unviable — this skill addresses ordinary difficulty and discouragement, not a signal to ignore clear disconfirming evidence.
- When continuing exposes you to risk of genuine, irreversible harm (financial ruin, health, safety) beyond what the pursuit's potential upside can justify — persistence has to be weighed against the actual magnitude of downside risk, not applied unconditionally.
- For short-duration tasks with no meaningful attrition dynamic — the mechanism this skill targets specifically depends on a long-duration pursuit where others exit over time, thinning the competing population; it doesn't apply to a single, brief decision point.
