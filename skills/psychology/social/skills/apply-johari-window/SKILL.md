# Apply Johari Window

Map what is known to yourself and known to others into four distinct quadrants — open, blind, hidden, and unknown — and deliberately use two different corrective actions (soliciting feedback to shrink the blind area, and voluntary disclosure to shrink the hidden area) because self-awareness gaps and undisclosed information are two distinct problems that each require a different action to resolve.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Why best:** Luft and Ingham's specific contribution is not a vague call to "communicate more openly" — it's a structural distinction between two different categories of interpersonal information gap that get conflated in ordinary practice. The blind area (known to others, not known to self) can only be reduced by soliciting feedback — no amount of self-reflection alone closes it, because it is specifically the information others have that you don't. The hidden area (known to self, not known to others) can only be reduced by voluntary disclosure — no amount of others observing you closes it, because it is specifically the information you haven't shared. Treating these as the same problem, or trying to fix one with the technique that only fixes the other, is a specific, identifiable failure mode this model exists to prevent.

**Luft & Ingham (1955):** The originating model, developed for group-dynamics training, formalized interpersonal awareness as a two-by-two grid: the Open area (known to self and known to others), the Blind area (known to others, not known to self), the Hidden area (known to self, not known to others), and the Unknown area (not known to either). The model's practical claim is that deliberately soliciting honest feedback specifically shrinks the Blind quadrant by moving information from it into the Open quadrant, while deliberate, appropriate self-disclosure specifically shrinks the Hidden quadrant the same way — and that a larger Open quadrant, reached through these two distinct actions, is associated with more effective interpersonal and team functioning.

**Adopted by:** The Johari Window has been widely adopted in group-dynamics training, organizational-development practice, and leadership-coaching curricula since its introduction, and remains standard material in team-building and interpersonal-effectiveness training programs.
**Impact:** The model's specific, durable contribution to feedback and self-awareness practice is the two-by-two structural distinction itself — separating "I don't know how others perceive me" (Blind) from "others don't know something relevant about me" (Hidden) as different problems needing different actions — which has made it a standard diagnostic frame in team and leadership development for identifying which specific corrective action (soliciting feedback versus disclosing) actually applies to a given awareness gap.

## Steps

1. **Map the current state of a specific relationship or team dynamic across the four quadrants before choosing a corrective action.** Identify concretely what's likely Open (shared, mutually understood), what might be Blind (others may see something about you that you don't), what's Hidden (you're aware of something relevant you haven't shared), and acknowledge the Unknown exists without assuming you can map it directly.

2. **To reduce the Blind area, solicit honest feedback specifically — self-reflection alone cannot close this quadrant, since by definition it's information you don't have access to without someone else providing it.** Ask direct, specific questions rather than open-ended ones that are easy to deflect ("is there anything I do in meetings that comes across differently than I intend?" rather than "any feedback for me?").

3. **To reduce the Hidden area, disclose relevant information voluntarily and appropriately — soliciting more feedback from others cannot close this quadrant, since it is specifically information only you currently hold.** Choose disclosures that are relevant to the working relationship or team function, not disclosure for its own sake.

4. **Distinguish which quadrant is actually the source of a given misunderstanding before choosing your corrective action.** If the recurring issue is "I keep being surprised by how others perceive something I do," that's a Blind-area problem requiring solicited feedback; if the issue is "others keep making assumptions because I haven't shared something relevant," that's a Hidden-area problem requiring disclosure — using the wrong corrective action for the actual gap wastes effort without closing it.

5. **Create structured, recurring opportunities for both feedback-soliciting and disclosure, rather than treating either as a one-time event.** A single feedback session or one disclosure doesn't durably shrink the respective quadrant; both benefit from being built into an ongoing practice (regular check-ins, structured retrospectives) rather than isolated incidents.

6. **Calibrate the pace and depth of disclosure and feedback-seeking to the actual relationship and context.** Not all Hidden or Blind information is appropriate to surface in every relationship or setting; use judgment about what's relevant and appropriate to the working context, rather than treating maximal disclosure as inherently better.

## Rules

- Never attempt to reduce the Blind area through self-reflection alone — it specifically requires soliciting information from others, since it is by definition information you don't currently have.
- Never attempt to reduce the Hidden area by asking for more feedback — it specifically requires your own voluntary disclosure, since it is by definition information only you currently hold.
- Diagnose which quadrant is actually the source of a specific recurring misunderstanding before choosing a corrective action, rather than defaulting to the same action regardless of which gap is actually present.
- Build both feedback-soliciting and disclosure into recurring practice rather than treating either as a single, one-time event.

## Examples

**Team feedback session:** A manager notices recurring friction they can't explain and asks a trusted colleague for specific, direct feedback about how their communication style comes across in meetings — surfacing a Blind-area issue (a tendency to interrupt that others had noticed but never mentioned) that self-reflection alone had never revealed.

**Voluntary disclosure resolving assumptions:** A team member who has been working under significant personal stress, information they had kept private (Hidden), chooses to share relevant context with their manager — resolving a pattern of the manager making incorrect assumptions about a recent dip in engagement, which no amount of the manager observing or guessing had clarified.

**Correct diagnosis before action:** A team lead initially responds to interpersonal friction by asking for more feedback about their own behavior, but on closer examination realizes the actual gap is that a team member has withheld relevant information about a scheduling constraint (a Hidden-area issue on the team member's side) — redirecting the corrective action toward creating space for that disclosure rather than continuing to solicit feedback about their own behavior, which wasn't the actual source of the friction.

## Common Mistakes

- **Trying to reduce the Blind area through more self-reflection instead of actually soliciting external feedback.** This quadrant is specifically defined by information you don't have access to on your own.
- **Trying to reduce the Hidden area by requesting more feedback rather than actually disclosing.** This quadrant is specifically defined by information others don't have unless you provide it.
- **Treating a single feedback conversation or disclosure as a permanent fix**, rather than building both into ongoing, recurring practice.
- **Disclosing indiscriminately without judgment about relevance or appropriateness to the working relationship**, treating maximal openness as inherently better rather than calibrated to the actual context.

## When NOT to Use

- In relationships or contexts where the level of trust or psychological safety needed for honest feedback and appropriate disclosure doesn't yet exist — attempting this technique prematurely, before that foundation is built, can produce superficial or performative responses rather than genuine information exchange.
- For situations where the actual issue is a straightforward factual or process disagreement rather than an interpersonal awareness gap — not every team friction is a Johari Window problem, and misapplying this framework to a purely logistical disagreement adds unnecessary process.
- When soliciting feedback or making disclosures would violate genuine privacy boundaries or professional appropriateness — the technique assumes information that is legitimately relevant and appropriate to share within the specific working relationship, not an obligation to disclose everything.
